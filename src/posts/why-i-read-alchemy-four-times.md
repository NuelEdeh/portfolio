Rory Sutherland has a line I think about constantly: *"The opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea."*

He's talking about how logic is a terrible guide to human behaviour — and how the most powerful product decisions often look irrational on a spreadsheet but feel exactly right to users.

I first read Alchemy in 2020. I read it again while building Neat, then again during my first year at Cloudflare, then again recently. Each time I find something I missed.

## What changed in how I approach product

The biggest shift was moving away from optimisation as the default frame.

Most PM thinking starts from: *how do we increase conversion, reduce churn, improve retention?* These are fine questions. But they treat users as rational actors optimising for known preferences — which almost no human being actually does.

Sutherland's frame is different: *what would make this feel different?* Not better in a measurable sense — different in a psychological sense. Less anxiety-inducing. More trustworthy. More delightful.

At Neat, we stopped trying to explain our product's features and started trying to reduce the cognitive load of managing GitHub notifications. The product didn't change much. The first-run experience changed significantly. Retention improved.

At Cloudflare, I found myself thinking about why enterprise customers churn — and realising that the proximate cause (a missing feature, a billing issue) was almost never the real cause. The real cause was a feeling: *do these people understand what we're trying to do?* When I owned incident communications, I was really in the business of managing that feeling during its most fragile moments.

## The line I come back to most

"Logic can sell anything to nobody. Psychology can sell nothing to everybody."

I don't think product managers are trained to think this way. We're trained to look at data, identify problems, ship solutions, measure outcomes. All of that is necessary. None of it is sufficient.

The question I now ask at the start of every product decision: *what would this have to feel like for it to actually work?*

It sounds soft. It isn't. Getting that answer right is the hardest part of the job.
